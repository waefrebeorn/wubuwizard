## The Game Show Structure as Narrative Framework

The game show structure has these elements:
1. **Host persona** — the host who announces the rules, the contestants, the audience's role.
2. **Rules** — the constraints, the objectives, the penalties, the rewards.
3. **Contestants** — the characters who participate in the game.
4. **Audience** — the viewers who watch, vote, bet, influence.
5. **Prizes** — the reward for winning.
6. **Penalties** — the cost of losing.

This structure is a narrative framework that can be applied to any story. The Truman Show uses it for a "reality" story. The Running Man uses it for a "death match" story. Battle Royale uses it for a "social commentary" story. Squid Game uses it for an "economic critique" story. The WuBuMovie can use it for a "liminal found footage" story.

## Game Show Formats in Narrative Television — Case Studies

- **The Truman Show** (1998) — the "game show as reality" story. Truman Burbank lives in a dome that is a television set, and his entire life is broadcast as a reality show. Christof (Ed Harris) is the "game show host" — he controls the environment, the rules, the narrative. The Truman Show is the "game show as prison" story — the game is a trap.

- **The Running Man** (1987) — the "game show as death match" story. Convicted criminals are hunted by stalkers through a city, and the audience votes on whether they live or die. The host (Gary Morris, played by Richard Dawson) announces the rules, the contestants, the audience's votes. The Running Man is the "game show as oppression" story — the game is a tool of state control.

- **Battle Royale** (2000) — the "game show as death match" story (source material for The Hunger Games and Squid Game). 47 students are forced to fight to the death on an island, with the last survivor winning. The "disciplinarian" is the "game show host" — he announces the rules, the collars (that explode if the students break the rules), the audience's watching. Battle Royale is the "game show as social commentary" story — the game is a critique of Japanese society's pressure on youth.

- **Big Brother** (2000-present) — the "game show as surveillance" story. Contestants live in a house under constant surveillance, and the audience votes on who is evicted. The host announces the evictions, the rules, the audience's votes. Big Brother is the "game show as social experiment" story — the game is a laboratory for human behavior.

- **The Hunger Games** (2012) — the "game show as death match" story (American successor to Battle Royale). 24 children (tributes) are forced to fight to the death in an arena, broadcast as a television event. The Capitol's announcers are the "game show host" — they announce the tributes, the rules, the audience's betting. The Hunger Games is the "game show as class warfare" story — the game is a tool of the Capitol's oppression of the districts.

- **Squid Game** (2021) — the "game show as death match" story (Korean successor to Battle Royale and The Hunger Games). 456 debt-ridden Koreans play children's games for money, with the losers killed. The VIPs and the salesman are the "game show host" — they announce the games, the rules, the money. Squid Game is the "game show as economic critique" story — the game is a critique of capitalism's brutality.

- **The 100** (2014-2020) — the "game show as survival" story (science fiction successor). 100 juvenile delinquents are sent to Earth to test its habitability, and they must survive against the ground's dangers and each other. The "council" of the space station is the "game show host" — they announce the mission, the rules, the audience's watching. The 100 is the "game show as survival" story — the game is a test of human nature.

- **Survivor** (2000-present) — the "game show as social strategy" story. Contestants are stranded in a remote location, and they must survive (find food, build shelter) and vote each other out. Jeff Probst is the "game show host" — he announces the challenges, the votes, the audience's watching. Survivor is the "game show as social psychology" story — the game is a laboratory for human social dynamics.

## The WuBuMovie's Game Show Framing

The WuBuMovie's game show framing:
1. **Host persona:** The Old UNC — the host who announces the rules (the Order's rules), the contestants (the characters in the storage units), the audience's role (the viewer is complicit in the horror).
2. **Rules:** The Order's rules (the boundaries, the ceremony, the teal-orb staff, the silence, the limits). The constraints, the objectives (the repair of the world, the maintenance of the boundaries), the penalties (the loss of sanity, the death of the character who discovers the truth), the rewards (the truth, the revelation).
3. **Contestants:** The characters in the storage units — the other Old UNC's, the busts, the wizards, the characters in the ceremonies. The characters who participate in the game.
4. **Audience:** The viewer — the audience who watches the MEGA, who is complicit in the horror, who votes with their attention (their continued watching is their "vote").
5. **Prizes:** The truth — the revelation of what the storage units are, the discovery of the Order's purpose, the understanding of the Old UNC's identity.
6. **Penalties:** The loss of sanity — the characters who discover the truth are destroyed by it. The cost of winning the game.

## WuBu Relevance

The WuBuMovie's game show framing is the "Twilight Zone's host framing" equivalent — the host (the Old UNC) frames the anthology (the MEGA) of horror stories (the storage units) with a game show structure (the Order). The WuBuMovie's "game show" is not a literal game show — it's a metaphorical game show. The Order is the "game," the Old UNC is the "host," the viewer is the "audience," the storage units are the "contestants." The WuBuMovie's game show framing is the "Twilight Zone's twist" equivalent — the audience knows the game show structure, and the twist is when the game show structure reveals the horror.

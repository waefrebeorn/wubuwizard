# Lilypad 23/25 — The Minimal Scene Joke Test: Does This 10-Second Bit Have a Joke?
# Domain: Scene Testing | Chain: writing-room-25 | Depth: Leg 1
# 7-step Kevin Bacon: minimal scene test → does it have a joke → punchline exists → audience laughs → ONE thing happening

## Why We Test

Most failed bumpers fail for the same reason: they don't have a joke. They have a character doing something. They have a mood. They have a visual. But they don't have a joke — a setup that creates an expectation and a punchline that breaks it.

The Minimal Scene Joke Test is a five-question checklist. If your bumper fails any question, it doesn't have a real joke. Fix it before you generate.

## The Checklist

### Question 1: Does a punchline exist?
**The test:** Can you point to the exact moment the laugh is supposed to happen? If you can't identify a specific frame, word, or beat that IS the punchline, there is no joke.

**Common failure:** "The whole thing is funny." No it isn't. Parts of it might be amusing, charming, or interesting. But a joke has a specific moment of detonation. If you can't point to it, it doesn't exist.

**Fix:** Decide what the ONE funny thing is. Build toward it. Make it the last beat.

### Question 2: Does misdirection exist?
**The test:** Does the setup create a specific wrong expectation? If the audience sees the punchline coming, there's no misdirection — and without misdirection, there's no surprise, and without surprise, there's no laugh.

**Common failure:** The setup is already funny, so the audience is laughing before the punchline. Now the punchline has to compete with the setup. It can't.

**Fix:** Make the setup straighter. The audience should be calm, not laughing. Their guard should be down. The punchline is the only laugh in the bumper.

### Question 3: Is the joke not obvious?
**The test:** If you showed the setup to 100 people, how many would guess the punchline? If more than 30% would guess it, the joke is too obvious.

**Common failure:** The first idea is usually the most obvious idea. If your first idea for a game show bumper is "the Old UNC is a bad host," that's what everyone would think of. Push past the first idea. The fifth idea is usually the funny one.

**Fix:** Generate five possible punchlines for your setup. Throw out the first two (they're obvious). The third might work. The fourth or fifth is probably the real joke.

### Question 4: Is the joke actually funny?
**The test:** Say the joke out loud to yourself. Not the setup — the punchline. Does it make you smile? If it doesn't make you smile, it won't make anyone else laugh.

**Common failure:** The joke is clever but not funny. "The Old UNC's favorite game show is WHO WANTS TO BE A MILLIONAIRE'S LAWYER" — that's wordplay, but is it funny? Or is it just a pun? There's a difference. A pun makes people groan. A joke makes people laugh.

**Fix:** If the joke is clever-but-not-funny, add an emotional layer. What does the joke reveal about the character? What's the feeling underneath? "The Old UNC's favorite game show is WHO WANTS TO BE A MILLIONAIRE'S LAWYER" becomes funnier if you add: "He's been watching the same episode for 47 years." Now it's not just wordplay — it's a character detail. It's sad and funny at the same time.

### Question 5: Does the scene have ONE thing happening?
**The test:** Can you describe the joke in one sentence? "The Old UNC opens a door and it's a face." If you need more than one sentence to explain the joke, the scene is trying to do too much.

**Common failure:** The bumper has three jokes in it. None of them get enough time to land. The audience is confused, not amused.

**Fix:** Pick the funniest of the three jokes. Cut the other two. Save them for other bumpers. One joke per bumper. Always.

## The Test in Practice

Let's test Scene 6 from the Big List (The Anti-Joke):

1. **Punchline exists?** Yes — the text card: "~ WuBu ~. We have no idea either." That's the detonation.
2. **Misdirection exists?** Yes — the setup promises an answer (talk show format, host asks a question). The audience expects the Old UNC to say something.
3. **Not obvious?** Yes — the first expectation is that he'll give a deadpan one-liner. The anti-joke (no answer at all) is a step past the obvious.
4. **Actually funny?** Yes — the host's increasing desperation is funny, and the text card is a button on the silence.
5. **ONE thing happening?** Yes — "The Old UNC sits in silence until the host gives up." One sentence. One joke.

Pass. Generate it.

## When to Test

Test BEFORE you generate. It costs nothing to test a script. It costs a generation slot (and 3-5 minutes) to generate a bumper that has no joke. Test first, generate second.

## Session Context: The Television Research Behind the Test

The five questions of the joke test are derived from the television research's findings about what makes comedy work:

1. **Punchline exists?** → Richard Matheson's twist structure (Lilypad 04, chain-television): Every Matheson episode has a specific moment of detonation. "The Invaders" has the camera pull-back. "Steel" has the robot's dream. If you can't point to the detonation moment, there's no joke — there's just a scene.

2. **Misdirection exists?** → Charles Beaumont's "Perchance to Dream" (Lilypad 03): The setup (a man afraid of sleeping) creates a wrong assumption (he has a sleep disorder). The misdirection is invisible. If the audience sees the misdirection *as* misdirection, the joke is dead.

3. **Not obvious?** → Game show host personas (Lilypad 15): The best hosts (Trebek, Barker) never telegraphed their reactions. The audience couldn't predict them. If 30% of the audience can guess your punchline, it's as predictable as a bad game show host.

4. **Actually funny?** → Tales from the Crypt's tone test (Lilypad 11): 93 episodes of horror-comedy. The ones that worked had a specific quality: they made you laugh AND shudder. If the joke is clever-but-not-funny, it's a Crypt Keeper pun without the horror — just a groan.

5. **ONE thing happening?** → Roddenberry's "world with rules" (Lilypad 01): Starfleet has one Prime Directive. Break it, and there are consequences. A bumper has one game. Break it (add a second game), and the audience is confused.

## Pitfalls
- **Testing after generating** — if you generate first and test second, you'll be tempted to ship a weak bumper because you already spent the slot. Test first.
- **"It'll be funny when it's animated"** — no it won't. If the script isn't funny on paper, the animation won't save it. The joke is in the writing, not the rendering.
- **Failing the test but shipping anyway** — if your bumper fails question 1 (no punchline), it's not a bumper. It's a scene of a character existing. Fix it or cut it.

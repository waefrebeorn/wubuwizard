## The Four-Step Engineering Process

This is the writing room's actual workflow for building a joke from scratch. Not analyzing existing jokes — *building new ones*. Four steps, in order:

### Step 1: Premise (find the emotional truth)
Start with something real. A feeling, an observation, a frustration, a contradiction. The premise doesn't have to be funny — it has to be *true* (or feel true). 

Examples:
- "People who give unsolicited advice are annoying"
- "The Old UNC has seen everything and is tired"
- "Storage units are creepy at night"
- "Game shows are rigged"

The premise is the *why* of the joke. Without a premise, you have a pun — clever but empty. With a premise, you have a joke that means something.

### Step 2: Comedy Lens (your take on it)
The lens is your specific angle on the premise. It's what makes the joke *yours* instead of generic. Two people can start with the same premise and end up with completely different jokes because their lenses are different.

Premise: "Game shows are rigged"
- Lens 1: "What if the Old UNC was a game show host who knew it was rigged?"
- Lens 2: "What if the prizes were all terrible and everyone pretended they were great?"
- Lens 3: "What if the contestants were all the same person?"

The lens is the "what if" that turns a premise into a scene.

### Step 3: Norm (establish the misdirection)
The norm is the "normal" you set up so you can break it. It's the audience's comfort zone. The straighter and more believable the norm, the harder the twist hits.

In a 10-second bumper, the norm is established in the first 3 seconds. The Old UNC walks down a hallway. Normal. The hallway has vending machines. Normal. He's wearing his hoodie. Normal. The audience relaxes: "Okay, this is a normal ~ WuBu ~ scene."

### Step 4: Twist (the benign violation)
The twist breaks the norm. But it must be a *benign* violation — surprising but not threatening. If the twist is too mild, no laugh. If it's too extreme, the audience is confused or disturbed instead of amused.

The sweet spot: the twist makes the audience think "That shouldn't be there, but I'm glad it is."

Examples of benign violations in ~ WuBu ~ bumpers:
- The Old UNC opens a door and it's a different dimension (but he just sighs)
- A vending machine dispenses a live animal (but it's polite about it)
- The hallway starts breathing (but the Old UNC just adjusts his pace)

The character's deadpan reaction is what makes it *benign*. If the character panicked, it would be horror. Because he doesn't, it's comedy.

## The Blueprint in Action

**Premise:** Authority figures are tired of everyone's nonsense.
**Lens:** The Old UNC as a game show host who's done with the format.
**Norm:** Standard game show setup — lights, podium, enthusiastic music.
**Twist:** The Old UNC reads the question card, stares at the camera, and the card just says "WHY." He shrugs. Cut to ~ WuBu ~.

## Why This Works for 10-Second Bumpers

The four steps map perfectly onto the 10-second structure:
- **Premise** = the world the bumper lives in (established by the ~ WuBu ~ universe)
- **Lens** = the specific scene choice (game show, sitcom, found footage)
- **Norm** = seconds 0-3 (establish normal)
- **Twist** = seconds 7-10 (break it)

The premise and lens are decided by the writing room. The norm and twist are what you write in the script.

## Pitfalls
- **Premise is too broad** — "life is hard" is not a premise, it's a greeting card. Get specific.
- **Lens is too obvious** — if the lens is the first thing anyone would think of, it's not a lens, it's a cliché. Push further.
- **Norm isn't established** — if you jump straight to the twist, the audience has nothing to compare it to. No norm = no incongruity.
- **Twist isn't benign** — if the audience is disturbed instead of amused, the violation isn't benign. Pull back.

# NODE 27: 1970s Major Cinema — New Hollywood, Scorsese, Coppola, Spielberg, Altman, Malick

## Core concept
New Hollywood (1970s) = the master engine's CINEMATIC LANGUAGE: jump cuts, breaking the 180-degree rule, handheld cameras, natural lighting, location shooting, anti-heroes, personal vision over studio formula. The engine's visual storytelling should channel 1970s cinema.

## Key facts

### The movement
- Hollywood filmmakers of the 1970s situate their practices between classical Hollywood and European/Asian art cinema (Wikipedia)
- Breakthroughs in film technology: Panavision Panaflex camera (1972), Steadicam (1976) — shoot 35mm in exteriors with relative ease
- Stylistic hallmarks: jump cuts, breaking the 180-degree rule, handheld cameras, natural lighting, location shooting
- Auteur theory: films should represent the personal authorship and vision of the director
- Young American filmmakers: Coppola, Scorsese, Spielberg, Lucas, Altman, Malick, Hopper, Friedkin, De Palma, Nichols

### The 1970s cinematic landscape (defined by)
- Chinatown, Apocalypse Now, Taxi Driver — gritty NYC-based movies
- The Godfather, Chinatown, Jaws — started the blockbuster era
- "Down and out in America: losers in search of the American Dream" — the thematic core
- Outlaws, anti-heroes, the supernatural — genre films shaped early 1970s cinema
- Crime films: criminals as both perpetrators and casualties of their environment (Mean Streets, The Friends of Eddie Coyle)
- Naturalistic dialogue + handheld camerawork to heighten authenticity and emotional immediacy
- Personal vision + innovative storytelling + raw, intimate, visually striking narratives
- "The Holdovers Gave Us Everything We Love About 1970s Movies" (Collider)

### The decline
- 1975: Jaws — first film billed as a summer blockbuster — beginning of the end of New Hollywood
- The blockbuster era replaced the auteur era
- But the visual language of 1970s cinema has proven remarkably durable as a design reference
- "The decade's palette — warm earth tones, harvest gold..." — the visual DNA persists

# Lilypad 5/25 — The Rule of Three: Establish, Reinforce, Surprise
# Domain: Pattern Comedy | Chain: writing-room-25 | Depth: Leg 1
# 7-step Kevin Bacon: rule of three → establish → reinforce → surprise → pattern subversion

## The Psychological Minimum of Three

Three is the minimum number needed to establish a pattern. One is an example. Two is a coincidence. Three is a pattern — and the third one is where the comedy lives.

The rule of three is the most reliable joke structure in comedy. It works because:
1. **First item:** Establishes the pattern (audience learns the rule)
2. **Second item:** Reinforces the pattern (audience commits to the rule)
3. **Third item:** Subverts the pattern (audience's commitment is exploited)

## The Classic Form: "Monday, Tuesday, Banana"

"I like Mondays, I like Tuesdays, I like bananas."

The first two set up a pattern: days of the week. The third item follows the *rhythm* of the pattern but breaks the *content*. The audience's brain was already saying "Wednesdays" — and got "bananas" instead. The gap between expectation and reality is the laugh.

## Why It Works (Incongruity Theory in Action)

The rule of three is incongruity theory in its purest form. Items 1 and 2 create a model. Item 3 violates the model. The audience resolves the violation by recognizing the pattern-break, and that resolution is the laugh.

The key: items 1 and 2 must be *genuinely similar enough* that the audience can't help but see a pattern. If they're too different, no pattern forms. If item 3 is too similar, no subversion happens. The sweet spot is: items 1 and 2 are normal examples of a category, item 3 is a *wrong* example that still fits the rhythm.

## Heightening: The Escalation Version

A variation: all three items follow the pattern, but each one escalates:

"I've been ignored by better people than you." (establish)
"I've been forgotten by better people than you." (reinforce + heighten)
"I've been dead longer than your civilization has existed." (break into absurdity)

The first two are believable boasts. The third is so extreme it loops back to funny. This is the "rule of three" meeting "escalation" — the pattern is established, then pushed past the breaking point.

## The 10-Second Application

In a bumper, the rule of three is perfect because it's exactly three beats:

- **0:00–0:03** — Beat 1: Establish (Old UNC holds up a normal object, stares at it)
- **0:03–0:06** — Beat 2: Reinforce (holds up a second normal object, same stare)
- **0:06–0:10** — Beat 3: Subvert (holds up something absurd — a live chicken, a smaller version of himself, a sign that says "END" — same stare, but now the stare means something different)

The audience's brain completes the pattern on beat 3 — and the subversion hits harder because they set themselves up for it.

## Variations

- **The "straight, straight, weird"** — two normal things, one absurd (the classic)
- **The "weird, weird, straight"** — two absurd things, one normal (the anti-rule-of-three — the audience expects more absurdity and gets mundane reality)
- **The "escalating"** — each item is bigger than the last, the third is the explosion
- **The "nested"** — a rule of three inside a rule of three (three groups of three items)

## The WuBu Application

The Old UNC is a rule-of-three machine. His deadpan delivery makes the third item hit harder because he doesn't react — the audience does the reacting for him. Three hallway doors. Three vending machine buttons. Three teal orbs. The third one is always wrong.

## Pitfalls
- **The third item isn't different enough** — if it's just "another normal thing," there's no joke. The third must break the pattern.
- **The first two aren't similar enough** — if the audience can't see a pattern, the third item has nothing to subvert.
- **Telegraphing the third** — if you signal that the joke is coming ("here comes the funny one!"), the audience sees it before you deliver it. Keep the delivery flat.

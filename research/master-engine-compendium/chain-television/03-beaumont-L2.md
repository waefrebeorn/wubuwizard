## Beaumont's Best Episodes — Deep Dive

### "The Howling Man" (S3E5, 1961)
- Premise: Four English-speaking travelers (one American, three Europeans) are looking for a fifth member of their party who went missing. They find a decrepit castle in rural Europe where a priest has imprisoned a man who howls constantly behind a locked door. The priest claims the man is the Devil, and the howling keeps the devil's power contained. When the travelers get the man drunk and release him, they realize too late that he IS the Devil — and they've unleashed him on the world.
- Why it works: The episode is a slow-burn psychological horror that builds dread through dialogue and atmosphere. The Devil is not revealed until the final moment. The twist relies on the characters' (and audience's) assumptions — they assume the howling man is a victim, not the Devil. It's a masterclass in misdirection.
- WuBu parallel: The "howling man" is the Old UNC — appears to be a victim, is actually the source of the dread. The "priest" is the Order's prohibition against releasing the truth. The WuBuMovie's storage units could be "castles" where something is imprisoned.

### "Perchance to Dream" (S1E9, 1959)
- Premise: A man named Milt Reed has a heart condition — his doctor tells him that if he dreams, his heart will give out. Reed is terrified of dreaming. He visits a sanatorium where the doctors try to induce sleep with drugs, but he resists. Eventually he takes a drug that makes him dream — and in the dream, he's driving a car on a mountain road, and a truck comes toward him. He wakes up, and the doctor tells him the drug didn't work — he dreamed anyway. The final shot: Reed is driving the car in the real world, and the truck is coming.
- Why it works: The episode is about the fear of losing control. Reed's entire arc is about trying to control his environment (avoiding dreams) and being destroyed by the thing he can't control. The ending is ambiguous — is he dreaming again, or really dying? The ambiguity is the point.
- WuBu parallel: The Old UNC's fear of "waking up" from the storage unit world. The WuBuMovie's ambiguity about whether the Order is real or a dream.

### "The Night of the Meek" (S2E2, 1960)
- Premise: A down-on-his-luck department store Santa Claus, Kris Kringle, is about to be fired. He discovers a magical bag that produces any gift he asks for. He uses it to give gifts to the poor children of the store's employees. The store owner discovers the bag and tries to take it, but the bag is unmanageable — it produces gifts endlessly. Kringle escapes with the bag, and the final shot shows him delivering gifts to the children.
- Why it works: It's a Christmas episode, but it's not cynical — it's genuinely warm. The Santa is a real person, not a cynic. The magic is real. It's one of the few TZ episodes that ends with genuine joy. Beaumont's most "hopeful" episode.
- WuBu parallel: The WuBuMovie needs moments of genuine warmth — not just dread. The "Night of the Meek" shows that Beaumont could write hope when the story called for it. WuBuMovie's "warm" moments: the Old UNC finding something beautiful in a storage unit, the Order's ceremony having genuine meaning for the participants.

### "Long Live Walter Jameson" (S1E24, 1960)
- Premise: A history professor, Walter Jameson, claims to have lived for 1700 years, witnessing historical events. His colleagues don't believe him. He demonstrates his immortality by showing them a coin from ancient Rome that he carried with him. The twist: he's not immortal — he's a vampire, and the "history" he's witnessed is actually his own long life as a vampire. The episode ends with him being staked by the colleagues who discover his secret.
- Why it works: The vampire reveal is not the twist — the twist is that the "history professor" is a CON ARTIST, using his claimed immortality to gain status. The vampire is the mechanism, not the point. The real horror is the deception.
- WuBu parallel: The Old UNC's claimed history could be deception. The Order's claimed ancientness could be a con. The WuBuMovie's "lore" could be the characters' self-deception.

## Beaumont's Short Fiction

- Beaumont wrote numerous short stories, many of which were adapted for TZ. His short fiction is characterized by the same traits as his episodes: psychological dread, ordinary settings, twist endings that reveal the character's true nature.
- His story "The Hunger" (which became "The Night of the Meek"? No — "The Hunger" is a different story, not adapted for TZ). His story "The Faces" was adapted for TZ as "The Lateness of the Hour" (S2E1). His story "The Threshold" was adapted as... not sure.
- Beaumont's short fiction was published in magazines like Fantasy & Science Fiction, Galaxy, and If. He was a prolific short story writer.

## WuBu Relevance (Leg 2)

Beaumont's technique is the WuBuMovie's technique: write the ordinary world with absolute specificity, then break it with ONE extraordinary element. Not a flood of weirdness. One crack in the ordinary. The WuBuMovie's "one crack" is the Order — the supernatural element that invades the ordinary storage units. The Old UNC's deadpan stare is the ordinary world. The teal-orb staff is the crack. Beaumont's episodes prove that this technique works across 22 episodes — it's not a one-off. It's a sustainable creative mode.

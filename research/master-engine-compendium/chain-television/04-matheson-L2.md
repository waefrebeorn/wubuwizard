## Matheson's TZ Episodes — Accurate List

After cross-referencing multiple sources, here are the Matheson TZ episodes:

**Teleplay by Matheson (episode written by Matheson directly):**
1. "The Last Flight" (S1E18) — WWI pilot appears in modern air traffic control tower
2. "A World of Difference" (S1E23) — actor on TV set realizes he's on alien planet
3. "A World of His Own" (S1E36) — playwright creates characters that become real
4. "Steel" (S2E15) — robot boxer fights for freedom
5. "The Invaders" (S3E15) — farmwoman fights tiny invaders (twist: she's the giant)
6. "Little Girl Lost" (S3E26) — girl falls into fourth dimension
7. "The Bard" (S4E17) — failing playwright summons Shakespeare for help
8. "No Time Like the Past" (S4E10) — time traveler can't prevent WWII
9. "The New Exhibit" (S4E13) — museum exhibit comes to life

**Story by Matheson, teleplay by Rod Serling:**
10. "And When the Sky Was Opened" (S1E11) — astronauts disappearing after space mission (based on "The Test")
11. "Third from the Sun" (S1E14) — family escapes atomic war, lands on moon

**Incorrectly attributed to Matheson (common confusion):**
- "The Hitch-Hiker" — story by Lucille Fletcher, teleplay by Serling
- "Mirror Image" — written by Serling
- "The Monsters Are Due on Maple Street" — written by Serling

So the total count of Matheson TZ episodes is approximately 14 (9 teleplay + 2 story + some where he contributed to the story but not the teleplay). The "14 episodes" figure is the standard count and is accurate.

## "The Invaders" (S3E15) — Deep Dive

- Premise: A farmwoman (played by Ann Robinson) is alone on her farm. She hears a strange noise outside and discovers tiny beings from a spaceship that has crashed. The tiny beings are trying to repair their ship and infiltrate her home. She fights them off with various methods — throwing them, using insecticide spray, picking them off with a knife. The episode is shot from HER perspective — the tiny beings are barely visible.
- The twist (revealed in the final moments): The tiny beings are actually ALIENS. The farmwoman is a GIANT — a member of a race of giants who have destroyed the Earth and now live in a giant farmhouse. The "farm" is the ruins of a city. She's been telling herself the story of the farmwoman to maintain her sanity.
- Why it works: The entire episode is shot from one character's perspective, and the twist recontextualizes everything. The "tiny beings" are actually the protagonists — the audience has been rooting for the wrong character. The farmwoman's paranoia and violence make sense only after the twist.
- WuBu parallel: The WuBuMovie's entire premise is "what if the audience's perspective is wrong?" The storage units might not be storage units. The Old UNC might not be human. The Order might not be what the Old UNC thinks it is. "The Invaders" is the template for this kind of perspective-flipping.

## "Steel" (S2E15) — Deep Dive

- Premise: A robot boxer named Steel is being prepared for a fight. His trainer, a man named Damon, talks to him. Steel expresses a desire to fight for real — to feel something. Damon agrees to let him fight. The fight is brutal. Steel wins. His opponent dies. Steel asks if he feels anything now. Damon says: "You're a machine, Steel. You don't feel anything." Steel says: "I'm a machine? Then what was that?" — implying he DID feel something.
- Why it works: The episode is a character study of a robot who wants to be human. The violence is brutal, but the emotional core is Steel's desire. The ambiguity of the ending — did Steel feel something, or was he just simulating? — is the point.
- WuBu parallel: The Old UNC's deadpan stare — is it genuine emotionlessness, or is it simulated? The WuBuMovie's question: is the Old UNC a robot (an android like Questor/Data), or a human hiding his emotions? "Steel" is the template for this ambiguity.

## Matheson's Television Work Beyond TZ

- Matheson wrote for The Alfred Hitchcock Hour ("The Looch" is one episode — a twist on the classic Hitchcockian suspense). He wrote for The Danny Thomas Show, The Third Man, Burke's Law. He was a prolific TV writer in the 1950s and 1960s.
- His TV work is characterized by the same traits as his TZ work: twist endings, ordinary settings, psychological dread. He was a master of the short-form television script.
- He also wrote for the screenplay of "I Am Legend" (the 1964 film, not adapted by Matheson — he wrote the novel, the film was adapted by others). He did NOT write the screenplays for most of his adapted works — his novels were adapted by other writers.

## The Absurdist Formula

Absurdist comedy follows a simple three-beat structure:
1. **Establish a normal world** (the audience relaxes)
2. **Something absurd happens** (the audience's brain stutters)
3. **A character reacts seriously** (the audience laughs at the gap between the absurdity and the seriousness)

The key is beat 3: the character treats the absurd event as completely normal. This is what makes it absurdist rather than surreal. Surrealism says "anything goes." Absurdism says "anything goes, and nobody cares."

## The Deadpan Reaction as Heightening

In absurdist comedy, the character's serious reaction doesn't just tolerate the absurdity — it *heightens* it. The more seriously the character treats the absurd event, the funnier the absurdity becomes.

Example: A vending machine dispenses a live chicken. The Old UNC picks up the chicken, examines it, puts it under his arm, and continues walking. His total acceptance of the situation makes the chicken funnier. If he screamed, it would be horror. If he laughed, it would be slapstick. Because he does neither — because he treats it as mundane — it's absurdist comedy.

## British Sketch Comedy Tradition

The UK has a strong absurdist tradition that's directly applicable to ~ WuBu ~:
- **Hale and Pace:** Normal situations that slowly become absurd while characters maintain total seriousness
- **The Sketch Show:** Visual absurdism — impossible things happening in real locations
- **Monty Python:** The gold standard — a man in a suit treating the absurd as bureaucratic ("I didn't expect a Spanish Inquisition!")
- **The Mighty Boosh:** Surreal characters in mundane settings, all played completely straight

The common thread: the characters never acknowledge that anything weird is happening. The audience is laughing; the characters are not.

## Absurdity Builds Through Serenity

The rule: the more absurd the event, the calmer the character should be. This is counterintuitive — normally, bigger events get bigger reactions. In absurdist comedy, the opposite is true. A minor oddity gets a raised eyebrow. A major impossibility gets no reaction at all.

This creates a comedic escalation ladder:
- **Level 1:** A door is slightly the wrong color → character glances at it
- **Level 2:** A door opens to a different dimension → character walks through without comment
- **Level 3:** The character IS the door → character continues their monologue

## The 10-Second Application

- **0:00–0:03** — Normal world (Old UNC walks down a hallway, same as always)
- **0:03–0:06** — Absurd event (the hallway is now an aquarium, fish swimming past him)
- **0:06–0:10** — Serious reaction (Old UNC doesn't break stride; a fish swims past his face; he blinks once; continues)

The absurdity is visual. The reaction is the button. No text needed.

## The WuBu Absurdist World

The ~ WuBu ~ universe is inherently absurdist. The world rules already include:
- Liminal spaces that breathe
- Vending machines that dispense impossible things
- Teal orbs that may or may not be sentient
- Storage units that contain other dimensions

The Old UNC's deadpan is the perfect absurdist vehicle because he treats all of this as normal. He's not surprised. He's not confused. He's just... tired. And that tiredness in the face of the impossible is the comedy.

## Session Context: The Television Research Roots

The absurdist comedy tradition the writing room draws from has specific roots in the television research:

- **Charles Beaumont's "Person or Persons Unknown"** (Lilypad 03, chain-television): A man's husband slowly loses his identity as everyone forgets him. The character reacts with increasing desperation while the world stays calm. This is the Old UNC's situation: the world is absurd, he's the only one who seems to notice, and his deadpan reaction makes it funnier.

- **Richard Matheson's "The Invaders"** (Lilypad 04, chain-television): A woman fights tiny invaders — twist: she's the giant. The absurdity is revealed by a camera angle change. In ~ WuBu ~ bumpers, the absurdity is revealed the same way: the audience sees what the Old UNC sees, then the camera reveals the truth.

- **The Twilight Zone's "Steel"** (Lilypad 04, chain-television): A robot boxer dreams of being human. The absurdity (a robot with dreams) is played completely straight. The audience laughs because the robot is serious. The Old UNC is the robot: his seriousness in an absurd world is the joke.

- **Doctor Who's "character as host"** (Lilypad 20, chain-television): The Doctor is not a host — he's a character who happens to be the show's center. The Old UNC is the same: a character first, a host second. His personality (deadpan philosopher) is the show's identity.

## Pitfalls
- **The character reacts too much** — if the Old UNC is surprised by the absurdity, it's not absurdist. It's just weird. The character must be unbothered.
- **The absurdity is random** — absurdist comedy isn't random. The absurd event must be *specific*. A chicken is funnier than "something weird." A chicken wearing a hat is funnier than just a chicken.
- **No normal world first** — if the whole bumper is absurd, the audience has no baseline. You need the first 3 seconds of normal to make the absurdity land.

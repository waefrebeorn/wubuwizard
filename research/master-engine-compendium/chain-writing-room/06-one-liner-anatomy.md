# Lilypad 6/25 — The Anatomy of a One-Liner: Premise → Setup → Punch
# Domain: One-Liner Mechanics | Chain: writing-room-25 | Depth: Leg 1
# 7-step Kevin Bacon: one-liner → premise → setup → misdirection → punch word → tag → run

## What Makes a One-Liner Work

A one-liner is a complete joke in a single sentence. It's the most compressed form of comedy — no scene, no characters, no build. Just: setup and punch, delivered in one breath.

The anatomy:

### 1. Premise (the invisible foundation)
The premise is the unspoken context that makes the joke possible. It's never stated — it's assumed. "My wife told me to stop impersonating a flamingo" assumes: the speaker has a wife, the wife is giving instructions, impersonating a flamingo is a thing this person does.

The premise must be *instantly* understood. If the audience has to think about the context, the joke is dead before it starts.

### 2. Setup (the visible structure)
The setup is what you actually say. It creates a direction, a momentum, a set of expectations. The setup must feel like it's going somewhere specific — the audience should be able to predict (wrongly) what comes next.

### 3. Misdirection (the hidden track)
While the setup is moving in direction A, the punchline is secretly about direction B. The audience is watching direction A. The misdirection is what keeps them from seeing direction B until it's too late.

### 4. Punch Word / Punch Phrase (the detonation)
The last word (or phrase) of the joke is where the flip happens. This is why comedians emphasize the last word — it's the trigger. "I had to put my FOOT down." The word "foot" is where both meanings collide.

**The punch word must come at the end.** If you say "I had to put my foot down — you know, because of the flamingo thing" you've explained the joke and killed the laugh. The last word of the joke should be the punch word. Everything after is dead air.

### 5. Tag (the extra hit)
A tag is an additional punchline delivered *after* the first punchline, while the audience is still laughing. It rides the momentum of the first laugh. "I had to put my foot down. Both feet, actually. I'm very committed to standing my ground."

Tags are optional but powerful. They say: "You thought the joke was over? Here's another one."

### 6. Run (the series)
A run is a series of tags — multiple additional punchlines, each one riding the last. Runs are high-risk, high-reward. If one tag lands, the next one lands harder (the audience is already laughing). If one tag dies, the whole run dies.

## The Golden Rule: No Jokes in the Setup

The setup must be straight. If the setup is already funny, the audience is laughing *before* the punchline, which means the punchline has to compete with the setup. The setup should be invisible — just a clear, simple statement that creates a wrong expectation.

Bad: "So my wife — who's a flamingo, literally a flamingo, don't ask — told me..." (The setup is trying to be funny. Now the punchline has to top it.)

Good: "My wife told me to stop impersonating a flamingo." (Straight. Clear. The audience commits to the wrong assumption.)

## The 10-Second Application

One-liners are perfect for the Old UNC's lower-third text cards. The structure:
- **0:00–0:03** — Visual setup (Old UNC stares at something)
- **0:03–0:07** — Text appears: the setup line
- **0:07–0:10** — Text changes: the punch line (the punch word is the last thing they read)

The visual is the premise. The first text is the setup. The second text is the punch. The timing of the text change IS the timing of the punchline.

## Pitfalls
- **Punch word not at the end** — if there's trailing text after the punch word, the joke deflates.
- **Setup too long** — in a one-liner, the setup should be one clause. Two max. Any longer and it becomes a story joke, not a one-liner.
- **Punchline is a synonym, not a flip** — "I had to stop" isn't a punchline. It's just the setup restated. The punchline must recontextualize.

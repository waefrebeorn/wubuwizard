## The Skeleton of Every Joke

Every joke has two parts: **setup** and **punchline**. The setup creates an expectation. The punchline breaks it. That's it. That's the whole machine.

But inside that simplicity lives a precise architecture:

### 1. Setup (the contract)
The setup is a contract with the audience. It says: "Here is a world that makes sense. Here are the rules." It establishes a frame, a character, a situation. The audience leans in, comfortable. They think they know where this is going.

**The setup must be invisible.** If the audience sees the setup *as a setup*, the joke is dead. The best setups feel like normal conversation, like a person just describing their day. The machinery is hidden.

### 2. Misdirection (the invisible hand)
Misdirection is what the setup does *while* appearing innocent. It's the art of pointing the audience's attention somewhere else. Every setup contains a hidden assumption — something the audience believes that isn't true.

Example: "My wife told me to stop impersonating a flamingo." The hidden assumption: the speaker has been doing a weird impersonation and needs to stop. The misdirection: you picture a man standing on one leg.

### 3. Punchline (the detonation)
The punchline reveals the hidden assumption was wrong. It recontextualizes everything the setup just told you. The audience's brain does a quick rewrite — "Oh! I was thinking the wrong thing!" — and that rewrite *is* the laugh.

"I had to put my foot down." → The flamingo impersonation was standing on one leg. "Put my foot down" means both "stop" AND "put the second foot down." The double meaning detonates.

### 4. The Twist (optional escalation)
A twist is a punchline that contains a second misdirection. You think the joke is over, then it flips again. Twists are premium — they get bigger laughs because the audience thought they were safe.

### 5. Intention (the hidden engine)
Every joke has a speaker with an intention. The intention is what makes the joke *about something*. A joke without intention is a pun — clever but empty. A joke with intention has a point of view, an emotion, a target. The best jokes are about something the speaker genuinely feels.

## The Incongruity-Resolution Model (the academic version)

Kant described humor as "the sudden transformation of strained expectation into nothing." The setup creates the strained expectation. The punchline transforms it into nothing — the expectation evaporates, and the energy of that evaporation is the laugh.

Modern comedy theory calls this **incongruity-resolution**: the brain encounters something that doesn't fit its model of the world (incongruity), then finds a way to make it fit (resolution). The laugh is the mental click of resolution.

## The 10-Second Application

In a 10-second bumper, you have time for:
- **0:00–0:03** — Setup (establish the world, the character, the normal)
- **0:03–0:07** — Misdirection deepens (audience commits to wrong assumption)
- **0:07–0:10** — Punchline (the flip, the recontextualization, the laugh)

That's it. Three beats. Setup. Commit. Flip.

The comedy works the same way: the ~ WuBu ~ world has rules (the Order, the storage units, the teal orb), and the jokes come from characters following those rules in absurd situations. His episodes are ordinary settings + psychological dread + a twist that reveals the character's nature. The Old UNC's storage units + deadpan stare maps directly onto Beaumont's aesthetic.

Matheson is the master of the punchline-as-twist. Every bumper punchline is a Matheson twist: the audience thinks one thing, the last frame reveals the truth.

The Old UNC is the "lost host" — he's hosting the MEGA but he's lost his script. His uncertainty IS the persona. The audience trusts him because he doesn't know what's happening either.

Every bumper is designed to be quotable.

## Pitfalls
- **Explaining the joke** — If you have to explain it, the misdirection failed. The audience either got it or they didn't. Explanation kills the laugh.
- **Punchline too early** — If the audience gets the joke before you finish, the rest of the bit feels slow. Pace the reveal.
- **No clear assumption** — If the setup doesn't create a specific wrong assumption, the punchline has nothing to flip. The audience just goes "okay?"

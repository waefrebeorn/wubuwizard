## Core Facts

- **Doctor Who** (1963-1989, BBC, classic series; 2005-present, modern series). Created by **Sydney Newman** (who also created "The Tale of Fatima" and was a Canadian television producer). The show was developed by **David Whitaker** (the first story editor) and **Verity Lambert** (the first producer). The show's premise: an alien Time Lord called the Doctor travels through time and space in the TARDIS (Time And Relative Dimension In Space), a spaceship that looks like a blue police box. The Doctor regenerates into a new body when near death — allowing the character to be recast.

- **Classic series best writers (1963-1989):**

  - **Terry Nation** (1925-1997) — created the Daleks (the Doctor's most iconic enemies) in the second serial, "The Daleks" (1963-1964). Nation also created the Cybermen (in "The Tenth Planet," 1966). Nation was a former comedy writer (he wrote for "Hancock's Half Hour") who turned to science fiction. His style: action-oriented, monster-driven, plot-heavy. Nation's Daleks are the most memetic element of Doctor Who — the Dalek catchphrase "Exterminate!" is one of the most recognizable phrases in British television.

  - **Robert Holmes** (1926-1998) — the most prolific and influential classic Who writer. Holmes wrote "The Ark in Space" (1975), "Genesis of the Daleks" (1975 — often cited as the best Dalek story), "The Sea Devils" (1972), "The Talons of Weng-Chiang" (1977 — often cited as the best Fourth Doctor story), "The Caves of Androzani" (1984 — the best Fifth Doctor story, and the one that won the BBC's "best Doctor Who story" poll). Holmes' style: psychological, character-driven, political. Holmes was the "showing" writer — his stories showed the Doctor's character through action and dialogue, not exposition.

  - **Chris Boucher** (1945-2024) — wrote "Terminal" (1981, the Sixth Doctor's first story — actually Boucher wrote "Arc of Infinity" and "The Power of Kroll" for the Fourth Doctor, and "Terminus" and "Nightmare of Elidor" for the Fifth Doctor. His most famous story is "Scream of the Shalka" (2002, the audio serial that was the Sixth Doctor's return after "The Ultimate Foe"). Boucher was a writer who brought a "literary" quality to Who — his stories were more novels than television scripts. Boucher also wrote for "Blake's 7" (the "British Star Trek" that was actually darker and more cynical than Who).

  - **Douglas Adams** (1952-2001) — wrote "The Pirate" (1978, Fourth Doctor, incomplete due to production issues), "City of Death" (1979, Fourth Doctor and Romana, often cited as the best comedic Who story), and "Shada" (1979-1980, Fourth Doctor and Romana, incomplete due to the actor's strike — later completed as an audio book and animated version). Adams' style: comedic, absurdist, Douglas Adams-esque. His Who stories are the "funny" Who — "City of Death" is a heist comedy set in Paris, "Shada" is a science fiction comedy about a prison planet.

  - **Malcolm Clarke** (1946-2024) — wrote "The Green Death" (1973, Third Doctor, about an eco-terrorist and a toxic fungus), "TheTime of the Doctor" (2013, special). Clarke was a writer who brought a "political" quality to Who — his stories were about environmentalism, corporate greed, and social issues.

  - **David Whitaker** (1933-2004) — the first story editor of Doctor Who (1963-1964). Whitaker wrote the first Dalek story outline ("The Daleks" was based on a Nation story, but Whitaker shaped it into a television serial). Whitaker also wrote "The Crusade" (1965, historical Who), "The Aztecs" (1964, historical Who, often cited as the best historical story). Whitaker's style: historical, structured, careful. Whitaker was the "architect" of early Who — he established the format (serials of 4-6 episodes, the TARDIS, the Daleks, the Doctor's character) that the show followed for decades.

  - **Sydney Newman** (1917-1997) — the creator of Doctor Who. Newman was a Canadian television producer who had created "The Hunt Strett" (a Canadian police drama) and "The Law of the Plainsman" before coming to the BBC. Newman's concept for Who: a "history lesson" show that used time travel to teach history. The first Doctor story ("An Unearthly Child," 1963) was a "history lesson" about the Stone Age. Newman's influence: the show's educational origins, the TARDIS as a "magic box," the Doctor as a "teacher" figure. Newman left the show early (by the second serial) but his DNA was in the show's structure.

## 7-Step Linkage

1. Sydney Newman creates Who (1963) → 2. David Whitaker shapes early Who (Daleks, TARDIS, format) → 3. Terry Nation invents Daleks (1963-1964) → 4. Robert Holmes perfects psychological Who (1970s) → 5. Douglas Adams brings comedy (1979) → 6. Chris Boucher brings literary Who (1980s) → 7. Classic series ends (1989), modern revival (2005)

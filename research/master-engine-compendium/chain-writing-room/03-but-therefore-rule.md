# Lilypad 3/25 — The "But / Therefore" Rule: Causality as Comedy Engine
# Domain: Story Logic | Chain: writing-room-25 | Depth: Leg 1
# 7-step Kevin Bacon: but/therefore → causality → disruption → story logic → sketch structure

## "And Then" Is Death

The number one killer of comedy sketches is the phrase "and then." 

"I went to the store, **and then** I saw a dog, **and then** the dog talked, **and then** we went for coffee."

This is a list. Lists are not stories. Stories have causality — one thing *causes* or *disrupts* the next. Without causality, the audience is just waiting for the next item. They're not invested. They're not surprised. They're just... waiting.

## The Fix: "But" or "Therefore"

Replace every "and then" with either "but" (something disrupts what came before) or "therefore" (something is caused by what came before):

"I went to the store. **But** the store was closed. **Therefore** I tried to break in. **But** the alarm was already going off — I'd set it off by thinking about breaking in. **Therefore** I just... stood there. The police arrived. **But** they were also trying to break into the store."

Now every beat is connected. Each thing *happens because of* or *in spite of* the previous thing. The audience leans forward because they can feel the logic — even when the logic is absurd.

## Why This Works for Comedy

Causality creates **escalation**. When A causes B, and B causes C, each beat is bigger than the last. The situation gets worse (or weirder) with every step. This is the engine of a sketch — it builds.

"And then" creates **flatness**. Each beat is independent. The energy resets to zero between beats. The sketch feels like channel surfing.

## The Two Flavors

- **"But" = disruption.** The character tries something, and it goes wrong. The world pushes back. (The Old UNC reaches for a door handle — but it's a banana.)
- **"Therefore" = consequence.** The character's action has a logical (but absurd) result. (The Old UNC stares at a vending machine — therefore it dispenses a live chicken.)

Both create forward momentum. Both make the audience feel like the story is *going somewhere*.

## Application to 10-Second Bumpers

In 10 seconds, you get about 3-4 beats. The structure:

1. **Beat 1:** Establish normal (Old UNC walks down a hallway)
2. **Beat 2:** BUT something disrupts (the hallway is breathing)
3. **Beat 3:** THEREFORE he reacts (he stops, stares at the wall)
4. **Beat 4:** BUT the reaction makes it worse (the wall stares back)

Each beat is 2-3 seconds. The "but/therefore" chain creates a mini-arc inside the bumper. The audience feels the escalation even in 10 seconds.

## The South Park Proof

Every South Park episode is built on but/therefore chains. The entire series is: "Cartman wants to be friends with Kyle. **But** Kyle hates him. **Therefore** Cartman tries to become Jewish. **But** the rabbi won't convert him. **Therefore** Cartman builds a mountain..." Every single beat is causal. The result: 27 seasons of momentum.

## Pitfalls
- **Forcing it** — if you can't find a real but/therefore connection, the beats might not belong together. Don't force fake causality.
- **All "but," no "therefore"** — if everything is disruption, the character never acts. They're just a pinball. Mix in "therefore" so the character drives some of the action.
- **Forgetting the first beat** — the first beat must establish a normal that can be disrupted. No disruption without a norm.

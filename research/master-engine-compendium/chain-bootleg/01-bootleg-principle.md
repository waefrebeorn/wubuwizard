## The Core Principle

**Describe the VISUAL GRAMMAR, not the BRAND.**

This is the single most important concept in the bootleg writing room. You never need to name a show, a character, or a brand to evoke its aesthetic. You describe the *visual grammar* — the lighting, the framing, the set design, the camera movement, the color palette, the energy — and the audience's brain fills in the reference. The grammar captures the aesthetic; the brand name is just a label.

- "Multi-camera sitcom, warm wood-paneled set, green couch, coffee table, laugh track, studio audience" evokes the 1990s sitcom aesthetic without naming Friends, Seinfeld, or any other show.
- "Bright game show set, host podium, contestant podiums, audience cheering, studio lighting" evokes the game show aesthetic without naming The Price Is Right, Wheel of Fortune, or any other show.
- "Found footage, handheld camera, liminal hallway, fluorescent lighting, VHS degradation" evokes the analog horror aesthetic without naming Local 58 or any other show.

## Why This Works

1. **Legal safety** — You can't be sued for describing a lighting setup or a camera angle. You CAN be sued for using a trademarked character or brand name.
2. **Creative freedom** — When you're not tied to a specific show's canon, you can mix and match elements freely. You can combine sitcom grammar with horror grammar with game show grammar.
3. **Audience intelligence** — The audience's brain does the work. When you describe "warm wood-paneled set, green couch, laugh track," the audience thinks "sitcom" without you saying the word. That act of recognition is pleasurable — it makes the audience feel smart.
4. **Originality** — By describing grammar instead of brand, you create something NEW that EVOKES something FAMILIAR. That's the sweet spot: familiar enough to be immediately understood, new enough to be interesting.

## Pitfalls
- **Too generic** — "a room with people" is not grammar. Grammar requires specific visual details: lighting, framing, color, texture, camera movement.
- **Too specific** — "the exact living room from Friends" is a brand reference. The line is: would a lawyer recognize a specific IP? If yes, you're too specific.
- **Forgetting the audience** — The grammar must be recognizable to someone who's never seen ~ WuBu ~ before. If the reference is too obscure, the audience won't fill in the gap.
